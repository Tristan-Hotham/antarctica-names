Who named Antarctica?

Antarctica has never had a native people, yet it has more than twenty thousand place names, almost all given in the last two centuries by two dozen nations at once. I mapped {{source.features|int}} of them: who named each one, when, and after whom.

Every other continent was named by the people who lived on it, over thousands of years, in languages long since lost. Antarctica was named in a hurry, by strangers, mostly within living memory. Sealers and naval expeditions gave the first names in the 1820s and 1840s; the explorers of the heroic age added more; and then, from the 1940s, aircraft photographed the interior and survey teams found tens of thousands of mountains, glaciers and nunataks that needed something to be called. The names went to the people who were there, to the people who paid, to ships and aircraft, to kings and queens, and sometimes to what a rock looked like.

The Scientific Committee on Antarctic Research (SCAR) keeps a Composite Gazetteer of Antarctica that brings together the national lists of {{source.gazetteers|int}} countries: {{source.rows|int}} names for {{source.features|int}} features once the same feature listed by several countries is counted once. Most entries carry a short narrative that says who named the feature and after what. I read those narratives with a set of rules, checked them by hand where it mattered, and linked the people to Wikidata to find where they were born. The question is simple. Whose continent is it, on the map?

    02 · Who named itAmerica and Britain, then everyone else

    The United States named {{nation_n.United States.n|int}} features ({{nation_n.United States.pct|pc}}), most through its Advisory Committee on Antarctic Names, which since 1947 has named the features found by American aircraft, ships and field parties. The United Kingdom named {{nation_n.United Kingdom.n|int}} ({{nation_n.United Kingdom.pct|pc}}), above all through its Antarctic Place-names Committee and the surveys of the Falkland Islands Dependencies Survey and the British Antarctic Survey on the Antarctic Peninsula. Then come Norway ({{nation_n.Norway.n|int}}), Bulgaria ({{nation_n.Bulgaria.n|int}}), Australia ({{nation_n.Australia.n|int}}), New Zealand ({{nation_n.New Zealand.n|int}}) and Russia ({{nation_n.Russia.n|int}}).

    A name in a country's gazetteer is not always that country's name. The SCAR gazetteer holds {{source.rows|int}} entries because most features are listed by several countries: {{source.multi_pct|pc}} appear in more than one national list. The Russian list, for example, holds {{gaz.2.n|int}} entries, most of them transliterations of names given by others. I count each feature once, for the nation that named it first. For {{source.nation_src.narrative|int}} features the narrative says who named it; for {{source.nation_src.only gazetteer|int}} only one national list holds the feature; for {{source.nation_src.discoverer|int}} I take the expedition the narrative says discovered or charted it; for {{source.nation_src.earliest approval|int}} the list that approved it first, the weakest of these rules; {{not_known|int}} remain unknown. I read the attribution of every land, coast, sea and ice shelf by eye and corrected {{source.nation_src.override|int}}.

    03 · ClaimsNaming as a claim

    Seven countries claim slices of Antarctica, frozen but not given up under the Antarctic Treaty of 1959. Naming a place has always been a way of saying it is yours, and the claimants mostly name inside their own claims: {{nation_n.Argentina.own_claim_pct|pc1}} of Argentina's names, {{nation_n.Australia.own_claim_pct|pc}} of Australia's and {{nation_n.Norway.own_claim_pct|pc}} of Norway's lie in their own sectors. But they do not name most of what they claim. Australia named {{claim_n.Australia.own_pct|pc}} of the features in the Australian Antarctic Territory and Norway {{claim_n.Norway.own_pct|pc}} of Dronning Maud Land. The United States, which claims nothing, named {{claim_n.New Zealand.top.0.pct|pc}} of the features in New Zealand's Ross Dependency and {{claim_n.Unclaimed.top.0.pct|pc}} of unclaimed Marie Byrd Land. On the Antarctic Peninsula, claimed three times over, Britain named {{claim_n.Argentina.top.0.pct|pc}} of the features inside the Argentine claim, and Argentina only {{claim_n.Argentina.own_pct|pc1}}.

    04 · WhenThe great naming of the 1960s

    Only {{eras.1.n|int}} of the dated names carry a date from the heroic age of exploration (1897 to 1922), the era of Amundsen, Scott, Shackleton and Mawson, though many more were given then and are dated here by a later approval. The flood came later. After the Second World War, aerial photography from American, British, Norwegian and Australian expeditions revealed whole mountain ranges, and the International Geophysical Year of 1957 to 1958 put permanent stations on the continent. Between 1960 and 1979 {{eras.5.n|int}} features were named, {{eras.5.pct|pc}} of all dated names; the busiest decade was the {{peak_decade|raw}}s with {{peak_decade_n|int}}. Naming has not stopped: {{since2000_pct|pc}} of dated names were given in 2000 or later, {{bulg_since2000_pct|pc}} of them by Bulgaria.

    05 · After whatPeople, ships, shapes and Bulgarian towns

    For {{source.stated|int}} features ({{source.stated_pct|pc}}) the narrative says what the name means. Of those, {{kind.person.pct_stated|pc}} honour a person, {{kind.descriptive.pct_stated|pc}} describe the place (its shape, colour or something that happened there), {{kind.place.pct_stated|pc}} are named after a place elsewhere, {{kind.association.pct_stated|pc}} take the name of a nearby feature and {{kind.ship.pct_stated|pc}} honour a ship. Nations name very differently. {{nation_n.United States.person_pct|pc}} of American names whose origin is recorded honour a person, and {{nation_n.United Kingdom.person_pct|pc}} of British ones. Norway and Japan describe: {{nation_n.Norway.descriptive_pct|pc}} of Norwegian names and {{nation_n.Japan.descriptive_pct|pc}} of Japanese ones are descriptive, like the Norwegian Skakktoppen (the crooked peak) or the Japanese Mikazuki Iwa (crescent rock). Bulgaria, which began naming in earnest in the 2000s, names after home: {{nation_n.Bulgaria.place_pct|pc}} of its names are towns, villages, rivers and mountains in Bulgaria.

    06 · Who is honouredCooks, pilots and patrons

    The people on Antarctica's map are not mostly famous. The commonest surnames among the features named after a person are Smith ({{surnames.0.person|int}}), Davis ({{surnames.1.person|int}}), Brown ({{surnames.2.person|int}}), Johnson ({{surnames.3.person|int}}) and Wilson ({{surnames.4.person|int}}), because the American and British committees honoured the people who did the work: the scientists, the surveyors, the aircrew and the station staff. Reading what the narratives say about each person, {{roles.All.science.pct|pc}} were scientists or surveyors and {{roles.All.military.pct|pc}} naval or military personnel. {{roles.United States.military.pct|pc}} of the people on American names served in the US Navy or another service, mostly in the air squadrons of Operation Deep Freeze; {{roles.Australia.support.pct|pc}} of the people on Australian names were station staff such as cooks, radio officers and weather observers. Bulgaria honours its own history: {{roles.Bulgaria.royal.pct|pc}} of its people are tsars, khans and politicians, and {{roles.Bulgaria.arts.pct|pc}} writers, artists and composers.

    07 · Where they were bornA European continent

    Wikidata links {{source.features_with_person_wd|int}} Antarctic features to {{people.n|int}} people, {{people.located|int}} of them with a known birthplace. {{people.europe_pct|pc}} were born in Europe and {{people.cont.North America.pct|pc}} in North America; Oceania has {{people.cont.Oceania.n|int}}, Asia {{people.cont.Asia.n|int}}, South America {{people.cont.South America.n|int}} and Africa {{people.cont.Africa.n|int}}. By modern country the United Kingdom leads ({{people.top.0.n|int}}), then the United States ({{people.top.1.n|int}}), France ({{people.top.2.n|int}}) and Norway ({{people.top.3.n|int}}). Some nations look abroad more than others: of the people on British names with a known birthplace, {{home_n.United Kingdom.home_pct|pc}} were born in the United Kingdom, against {{home_n.Norway.home_pct|pc}} of the people on Norwegian names born in Norway. The most honoured in Wikidata are Roald Amundsen ({{people.top_people.0.n|int}} features), Ernest Shackleton and Douglas Mawson ({{people.top_people.1.n|int}} each).

    08 · WomenWives, daughters and queens

    Of the {{kind.person.n|int}} features named after a person, {{women.n|int}} honour a woman ({{women.pct_person|f1}}%). They include some of the biggest names on the map: Queen Maud Land, Victoria Land, Marie Byrd Land and the Ronne Ice Shelf. But until recently the women on the map were there because of whom they were related to. Of the women honoured in names given before 1990, {{women.pre1990_rel_pct|pc}} were queens, princesses, wives, daughters, mothers or sisters of explorers and patrons. Since 1990 the share of women among people honoured has risen to {{women.since1990_pct|f1}}%, against {{women.before1990_pct|f1}}% before, and most of them are scientists and support staff honoured for their own work, from the glaciologist Julie Palais to the geologist Margaret Bradshaw: only {{women.post1990_rel_pct|pc}} are relatives or royals.

    09 · One place, many namesWhen nations disagree

    Most features in several national lists share one name, translated or transliterated: the Bellingshausen Sea is the Mar de Bellingshausen in Spanish and Bellingshausenhavet in Norwegian. But {{different.n|int}} features ({{different.pct|f1}}% of those listed more than once) carry names that differ by more than spelling or transliteration in different lists. Some are translations (the Seal Nunataks are the Robbeninseln in German and the nunataks Foca in Argentina's list); many are genuinely different names, mostly on the Antarctic Peninsula, where Argentina and Chile kept their own names for features Britain had named. Turn on the claims layer and click one: the popup lists every name.

    10 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Data. The SCAR Composite Gazetteer of Antarctica, as served by the Australian Antarctic Data Centre: {{source.rows|int}} names in {{source.gazetteers|int}} national gazetteers, plus undersea features named through the GEBCO undersea names committee. Names of the same feature share a common identifier; I count {{source.features|int}} features after merging {{source.merged|int}} pairs of identifiers that hold the same name at the same place. People and birthplaces come from Wikidata (CC0): features with coordinates in Antarctica and a "named after" statement, matched to the gazetteer by name within 25 km ({{source.wd_linked_pct|pc}} of Wikidata's Antarctic features matched).

    Reading the narratives. The naming nation, the naming year and what each feature is named after are read from the narratives by rules (published with the code): the phrase "named by" gives the committee, expedition or leader, mapped to a nation; the words after "named for", "named after" or "in honour of" give the honoree, sorted into a person, a ship, a place elsewhere, a description and so on. A first random audit of 100 features found about one in ten misread; after improving the rules a second audit of {{checks.audit_n|int}} found {{checks.audit_first_faults|int}} faults, which are fixed. An independent review then read the doubtful classes again and corrected {{checks.review_kind|int}} more readings (mostly people filed as "other"), found {{checks.review_women|int}} women the first pass had missed and removed {{checks.links_removed|int}} wrong Wikidata links; some people remain in "other", so the share of people is if anything an underestimate. Every possible woman (from titles, relatives, given names, Slavic surnames and Wikidata's sex) was read by hand: {{checks.women_read|int}} candidates, {{checks.women_yes|int}} confirmed. Features named after a couple or a family are not counted as honouring a woman. {{checks.bp_unmapped|int}} Wikidata birthplaces that are only a country or state are left off the map. Countries of birth are modern (Natural Earth). In the source, {{checks.uk_century_fixed|int}} British approval dates of 2001 to 2010 are stored a century early (1901 to 1910); I corrected them.

    Caveats. The narratives vary by country: the American, British, Australian and New Zealand ones are full, while many Russian, Argentine and Chinese entries have no narrative in English or none at all, so {{kind.unknown.pct|pc}} of features have no recorded meaning. The naming year is often the year a committee approved a name rather than the year it was first used, which pulls older names into the 1940s to 1960s. Claims are drawn as longitude sectors; undersea features ({{source.undersea|int}}, SCAR's sea-floor class) are counted as features but left out of every claim share. Wikidata covers only some of the people. Some readings will still be wrong; the data file lists the rule-based reading and the text it came from for every feature, so that anyone can check.
